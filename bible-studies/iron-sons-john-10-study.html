Jesus, the
Good Shepherd

John 10:9–18

Who does Jesus say he is—and what reasons does he give us to trust him?

John 10:9–18John 10:7–21

For elementary-age boys and their dads · A short route, with room to explore

StartContextObservationMeaningApplicationShare

Before you begin

A study to lead, not a list to finish

Use the questions that help the boys engage. Skip freely. The first goal is to open the Bible together, notice what Jesus says, and respond to him. Dads participate as learners too.

Leader’s working main point

Jesus is the good shepherd who saves and knows his people, willingly dies for them, and has authority to rise again.

Keep this in mind as you prepare. Let the boys discover the passage’s claims before you supply the summary.

If you have about 15 minutes

  1. Set the scene (2 minutes). Briefly tell the story from John 9 and choose two context questions.
  2. Read (3 minutes). Read John 10:9–18 aloud. Start at John 10:7 if helpful.
  3. Explore with dads (6 minutes). What does Jesus do for his sheep? How is that different from the hired worker? What does the difference show about Jesus?
  4. Share and respond (3 minutes). Which thing Jesus says gives you a reason to trust him—and why?
  5. Pray (1 minute). Thank Jesus for what you discovered.
More time? Use two dads + two sons

Establish context together, then split into groups of two dads and two sons for Observation, Meaning, and Application. Allow roughly 10–15 minutes to explore, then regather. Extend only while the boys are participating.

Dads can read aloud and write down discoveries. Boys can speak, point, circle repeated words, and ask questions. Give them time to think; do not let the discussion become a conversation between the two adults.

C · Context

Who is speaking—and what just happened?

John 9 shows varied responses to Jesus. John 10 continues with Jesus explaining who he is to people who disagree about him.

Jesus healed a man blind from birth. Religious leaders questioned the man and eventually threw him out. Jesus found him, and the man believed and worshiped. Nearby Pharisees challenged Jesus, and the conversation continued into chapter 10 (John 9:35–10:7).

The thread to notice: Who recognizes Jesus, and who can truly be trusted to care for God’s people? The story has already shown Jesus caring for someone whom the leaders rejected.

Context questions to choose from

Have the reference ready for dads. Read or summarize it, let the boys choose, and ask what helped them decide. This is an open-Bible discovery exercise; prior knowledge is not required.

Dad’s preparation notes · audience, disagreement, and shepherds
  • We know Pharisees are being addressed. John 9:40–41 leads into John 10:1 without announcing a new audience. We do not know everyone else present. Do not insist that disciples are explicitly named as listeners here.
  • “The Jews” is contextual. In John 10:19 it may refer to Jewish listeners more broadly or particularly the religious leaders. Pharisees were Jewish; Jesus and his followers were Jewish too. The phrase does not mean everybody except Romans, or that all Jewish people opposed Jesus. John 9:16 records division among Pharisees themselves.
  • Let the story show the different responses. The parents fear exclusion (John 9:20–23); the healed man believes (John 9:35–38); some listeners make a demon accusation and others challenge it (John 10:19–21). Ask what each person has seen and what is at stake. Do not assign everyone the same fear or skepticism.
  • The chapter also concerns spiritual sight. John 9:39–41 contrasts the man’s recognition of Jesus with the blindness of people confident that they see.
  • Shepherd language has a biblical history. Ezekiel 34:2–4 condemns leaders who fail to care for the flock. Ezekiel 34:11–16 promises that God will seek and shepherd his people; Ezekiel 34:23 promises one Davidic shepherd. Read these as background to Jesus’ claim, without turning the children’s introduction into a second study.
  • Keep the later scene distinct. John 10:22 introduces a new setting before the confrontation in John 10:30–38. It is useful for a separate discussion of Jesus’ divine identity, but is not the immediate next moment after the healing.

O · Observation

What does Jesus actually say?

Notice both what Jesus calls himself and what he says he does.

Read John 10:9–18 aloud. Give the boys one listening task: notice what Jesus says about himself. Briefly explain unfamiliar words such as pasture (a place to graze), fold (a sheep enclosure), and hired hand (someone paid to look after the sheep).

  1. What two things does Jesus call himself?Look at John 10:9 and John 10:11.
  2. What does Jesus say he does for his sheep?Gather answers across John 10:9–18. Ask boys to point to the words that helped them.
  3. What does the hired worker do when the wolf comes? Why?Look at John 10:12–13.
  4. What words or ideas do you hear more than once?Repeated references to his death are worth noticing. Boys do not need a perfect count.
More observation prompts and a dad’s answer guide
  • What does the thief do, and what does Jesus bring? (John 10:9–10)
  • Who knows whom? (John 10:14–15)
  • What will happen to the other sheep? (John 10:16)
  • What does Jesus say about his choice and authority? (John 10:17–18)

Listen for: door; good shepherd; salvation and life; care contrasted with abandonment; knowing his people; willingly giving his life; gathering one flock; authority to lay down his life and take it up again. Accept simple observations before asking what they mean.

M · Meaning

What does this show us about Jesus?

Understanding the pictures is a beginning. Ask what they reveal about Jesus and his relationship with his people.

  1. How is Jesus like a door?What does entering through him lead to? (John 10:9–10)
  2. What makes Jesus a good shepherd?Use his actions to explain your answer. (John 10:11–15)
  3. What does the difference between Jesus and the hired worker show?What matters to each of them when caring becomes costly? (John 10:11–13)
  4. What does Jesus mean by giving his life and taking it up again?Connect his words with his death and resurrection. (John 10:17–18)
Before application, ask for one sentence

“What is the main thing Jesus wants his listeners to understand about him?”

This can be spoken, with a dad helping. It checks understanding without adding another writing task.

Dad’s meaning notes · use as needed
  • Door and shepherd are complementary pictures. Jesus is the way to salvation and the caring shepherd of his people. He is not talking about a literal wooden door or literal human sheep. Do not force every feature of the imagery into a separate hidden meaning.
  • The sheep are his people. The image concerns belonging, being known, listening, and being cared for. Let John 10:14–16 explain the relationship.
  • His care is costly and committed. The hired worker flees because he does not care for the sheep. Jesus willingly gives his life for them. The point is the contrast in care, not a general condemnation of paid work.
  • His death is purposeful. John 10:17–18 anticipates death and resurrection, with authority and obedience to the Father. His death is not simply a plan going wrong.
  • The other sheep become one flock. John 10:16 is commonly understood in Christian interpretation as anticipating Gentile believers joining Jewish believers under Jesus. It does not teach that every religion is an equally saving path.
  • Abundant life is not a promise of constant ease. Read John 10:10 alongside the shepherd’s sacrifice. Do not promise wealth, comfort, or a life without danger.
  • Jesus is our Savior before he is our example. Begin with our need for his salvation and care. Reflecting his sacrificial love can follow, but “be brave and protect people” does not capture the passage’s central claim.

A · Application

Because this is who Jesus is, how should we respond?

One question to start with

Which thing Jesus says about himself here gives you a reason to trust him—and why?

Follow up: “When might you need to remember that this week?”

Application can be trust, gratitude, confession, comfort, or a concrete act of obedience. It does not always require inventing a new task. These are possible responses drawn from the passage’s claims, not extra commands the passage explicitly gives.

PassageWhat we discoverQuestion to explorePossible personal response
John 10:9–10Jesus brings salvation and life.Am I relying on Jesus to save me? What does this teach me about where true life is found?Trust Jesus for salvation. Thank him for the life he gives. For a boy who is unsure, invite an honest conversation with Dad about what trusting Jesus means.
John 10:11–13Jesus gives his life for the sheep.What does Jesus’ willingness to die tell you about his love? When might you need to remember that?Thank him for his sacrifice. When I question his love, remember what he willingly did. His care gives me a reason to trust him when following him is difficult.
John 10:14–15Jesus knows his sheep.How does being known by Jesus affect what you can bring to him?Bring my worries to him, trusting that I am personally known. Tell him about a specific fear instead of assuming that I am unnoticed.
John 10:16Jesus gathers other sheep into one flock.How might this shape the way we welcome another follower of Jesus who is different from us?Thank Jesus that his people extend beyond one background. Welcome fellow believers rather than treating our own circle as the only people who matter.

Let each person respond. Dads should name their own need for Jesus, not only identify something their sons should change. Invite the boys to speak honestly; do not assume that every child already trusts Jesus. Personal matters can stay between dad and son.

If you want a concrete example

A boy might say: “When I feel left out, I can tell Jesus about it and remember that he knows his sheep.” A dad might say: “When I worry that Jesus doesn’t care, I can remember his willingness to die and thank him for his love.”

Ask which verse supports the response. Avoid supplying a polished answer for a boy to repeat. If he offers only “trust Jesus,” invite him to explain what he has learned that makes Jesus trustworthy.

A further response: Having considered Jesus’ care for us, ask how we could reflect his costly love toward someone else. Keep that connected to reliance on him.

Regather & pray

Questions, light bulbs, and a response

Your familiar ❓ × 3   💡 × 2   ➡️ × 1 format can sit inside COMA. Use it as an optional target for each mini-group, not a quota for every child.

❓ Questions

What would you like to ask about the passage? A question can be worth asking even if you think you know the answer.

💡 Light bulbs

“We noticed ___ in verse ___. That helps us understand ___.” Connect a detail with its meaning.

➡️ Response

What will you trust, thank Jesus for, confess, or do in response? Each person can choose; share one as a group.

  1. First round: collect questions. Let groups read their questions without stopping to answer. If there are many groups, take one question per group per lap and skip repeats.
  2. Second round: share light bulbs. Ask what in the passage supports each insight. Notice whether discoveries help answer earlier questions.
  3. Before applications: clarify what matters. Resolve one or two questions essential to understanding Jesus’ words. Other questions can become something dads and sons investigate afterward. Postpone answers during the first round; do not make permanent confusion the goal.
  4. Third round: share responses. Invite boys to speak first and dads to participate personally. Do not require public disclosure of sensitive concerns.
  5. Close in prayer. Thank Jesus for the care, salvation, and willing sacrifice you have seen. Ask for help trusting him. Invite brief prayers without requiring every child to pray aloud.

A useful follow-up throughout: “What in the passage makes you think that?”

For the dads

Prepare with the surrounding story

Read John 9:1–41 and John 10:1–21 before leading, with Ezekiel 34:11–16 as optional background. Think about how different people respond to Jesus and why the contrast between caring and uncaring shepherds matters.

As the boys grow, they will meet people who disagree about Jesus. This study helps them explain reasons for trusting him from his words and actions. It does not need to become an adult apologetics debate.
